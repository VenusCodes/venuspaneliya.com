A startup with 3 developers should move faster than a startup with 1 developer.

In theory.

In practice, I’ve watched 3-developer teams ship slower than solo developers on the same scope of work.

Not because the individual developers were bad.
Because the coordination cost was eating their output alive.

Here’s the math nobody shows you.

1 developer

- Zero coordination overhead.

One person holds the full context.
Decisions happen in real time.

There’s no meeting to align on the API contract because the same person is building both sides of it.

2 developers

- 1 communication path.

Developer A and Developer B need to stay synchronized.

That’s manageable.
A quick daily sync, a shared document, a Slack channel.
Maybe 30 minutes of overhead per day.

3 developers

- 3 communication paths.

A-to-B.

A-to-C.

B-to-C.

Now you need:

- A standup.
- A shared task board.
- Someone making sure that the frontend developer’s assumptions about the API match the backend developer’s implementation.

That’s 1-2 hours of coordination per day across the team.

6 developers

- 15 communication paths.

The kind of team an agency puts together.

You need:

- A project manager just to manage the communication.

- Weekly planning meetings.

- Integration testing processes because six people building independently will produce six slightly different interpretations of the spec.

That project manager becomes the highest-value person on the team.

Not because they contribute code,
but because without them, nothing connects.
The overhead compounds.

Every new developer adds coordination cost that is invisible in the budget but visible in the timeline.

A project scoped at 8 weeks with 1 developer doesn’t become a 4-week project with 2 developers.

It becomes a 6-week project with 2 developers,
because 2 weeks of total effort went to coordination.
For early-stage startups,

Pre-product-market-fit.
Burning runway.

Building version one of a product that might pivot.

This math matters.
I’m not arguing that teams are bad.
I’m arguing that teams should be sized to the problem,
not to the budget or the instinct that "more people means faster."

If your entire product can be understood by one person,
it can be built by one person.

Faster, cheaper, and with zero coordination loss.

The right time to grow the team is when the product complexity genuinely exceeds what one person can hold in their head.

Not before.
